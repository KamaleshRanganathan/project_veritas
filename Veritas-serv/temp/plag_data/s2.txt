Our Solar System Explained

Our Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago when a huge cloud of gas and dust collapsed under gravity. At its heart is the Sun, a yellow dwarf star that supplies nearly all the energy for the planets.

There are eight official planets. The four inner ones—Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars—are made of rock and metal. Earth stands out because it has oceans of liquid water and an oxygen-rich atmosphere that supports diverse life forms. The outer four—Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune—are gas giants. Jupiter is the biggest planet and has a giant storm called the Great Red Spot that has raged for hundreds of years.

Many planets have moons. Earth has the Moon, Mars has Phobos and Deimos, and Saturn boasts more than 80 satellites, including Titan which has lakes of methane. Beyond the planets, the asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter, filled with rocky fragments left over from the system’s birth.

Farther out, the Kuiper Belt contains icy objects like Pluto, reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006. Even farther is the Oort Cloud, a distant shell of comets.

Comets develop glowing tails when they approach the Sun because heat turns their ice into gas. Studying the Solar System teaches us about how planets form and whether life could exist elsewhere. Space probes like Voyager and New Horizons keep sending back amazing data.
