The Solar System: An Overview

The Solar System is the gravitationally bound system consisting of the Sun and the objects orbiting it. Formed some 4.6 billion years ago from the collapse of a giant molecular cloud, it includes one star—the Sun—plus eight planets, countless moons, asteroids, comets, and dwarf planets.

At the center is the Sun, a G-type main-sequence star accounting for 99.86% of the system’s total mass. It delivers energy that powers weather, climate, and life on Earth via nuclear fusion in its core.

The eight planets divide into two types. The inner terrestrial planets—Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars—are rocky worlds with solid surfaces. Earth, third from the Sun, is the only known life-bearing planet due to its liquid water and protective atmosphere. The outer gas giants—Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune—are massive planets composed chiefly of hydrogen and helium. Jupiter, the largest, has a mass 318 times that of Earth and displays the famous Great Red Spot, a storm that has endured for centuries.

Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a zone of icy bodies that includes Pluto, classified as a dwarf planet. Farther out are the scattered disc and the hypothetical Oort Cloud, the origin of long-period comets.

The Solar System also features natural satellites. Earth has one large moon, while Mars has two small ones—Phobos and Deimos. The gas giants have extensive moon systems; Saturn alone has over 80 known moons, including Titan with its thick atmosphere.

Asteroids, mostly located in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, are remnants from the Solar System's formation. Comets, coming from the Kuiper Belt or Oort Cloud, show bright tails when approaching the Sun due to sublimation of ices.

Understanding the Solar System enables us to understand planetary formation, the potential for extraterrestrial life, and the future evolution of our star. Ongoing missions like NASA's Perseverance rover and the James Webb Space Telescope continue to expand our knowledge.
